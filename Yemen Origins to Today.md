## A NOTE ON METHOD, BEFORE WE BEGIN

This document follows one rule above all others: **we gather clues, we do not adopt theories.** Every claim below carries its pedigree — the institution, investigation, or document it comes from — because in a war as loud and as lied-about as Yemen's, the source is part of the fact. Where the evidence is thin, we say so. Where two credible sources disagree, we show both numbers. Where something is unverified, it is flagged as a lead, never presented as settled.

A second rule: **no side is taken.** The Saudi-led coalition, the Houthi movement, the governments of Yemen, the United States, Britain, France, the United Arab Emirates, Iran, and the armed groups of every stripe are all treated the same way — as actors whose documented acts are recorded, named, and weighed. If this document has a bias, it is only the bias of the dead: the 377,000 Yemenis — including 259,000 children under five — whose deaths are the spine of its modern chapters.

Third, a promise about style. Everything is explained. Technical terms are defined the first time they appear. Acronyms are always unwrapped. When the machinery of war, famine, or ancient irrigation needs an analogy, we use one — because the goal is that any reader, from any background, can follow Yemen's story from the Stone Age to last week.

# PART ONE — THE DEEP PAST

## 1. The Land Itself: Why Yemen Is Yemen

Long before any kingdom, any prophet, or any war, Yemen's story was written by its geography. To understand everything that follows — the incense wealth, the dam of Ma'rib, the coffee trade, the 21st-century famine — you must first understand the stage.

Yemen sits on the southwestern corner of Arabia, where the Red Sea meets the Gulf of Aden and the Arabian Sea. It has three great physical regions, and every era of Yemeni history plays out differently in each:

**The Tihamah** — a hot, humid, malaria-prone coastal plain along the Red Sea. This is the breadbasket-in-theory and the hell-in-practice: temperatures that can pass 45°C, flash floods, and thin soils. The great ports of the age of sail — Mocha, Hodeidah — stand on it.

**The highlands (the Sarawat)** — a dramatic escarpment of volcanic mountains rising to over 3,700 metres (Jabal an-Nabi Shu'ayb), catching enough rain — in good years — to terrace and farm. This is where Sana'a sits at 2,300 metres, where the famous mud-brick tower houses grow six and eight storeys tall, and where the independent streak of Yemeni politics has its roots: mountain peoples who were never easily ruled by coast, desert, or empire.

**The eastern plateaus and Hadhramaut** — the vast, arid interior running to the borders of the Empty Quarter, pierced by the extraordinary wadi systems (seasonal river valleys) of Hadhramaut, where cities like Shibam — Manhattan of the desert, its 16th-century mud-brick skyscrapers still inhabited — rose where water could be caught.

And around it all, the sea. Yemen commands the **Bab al-Mandab strait** — the "Gate of Tears," barely 30 kilometres wide — through which, today, roughly a tenth of the world's traded oil and a vast share of global container shipping must pass. Whoever holds Yemen's coast holds a chokepoint of the world economy. Every empire that ever came to Yemen came, in part, for that reason. The 2015–2026 war is no exception.

The land is also desperately poor in the one resource that matters most: **water**. Yemen is one of the most water-scarce countries on Earth, with renewable freshwater of roughly 80–86 cubic metres per person per year — the global water-poverty line is 500. The numbers behind the abstraction: the water table under the Sana'a basin has been falling six to eight metres every year; in besieged Taiz, households on the piped network received water once every 30 to 40 days at the height of the war; and the one serious engineering answer — piping desalinated water from the coast up over the mountains to Sana'a — was priced at an almost comical $10 per cubic metre, which is why the 21st century's answer turned out to be not engineering but solar panels on private wells. Ancient Yemen solved its water problem with collective genius; modern Yemen has largely failed to, which is why water runs through the modern catastrophe as surely as blood does.

The people. Yemenis are overwhelmingly Arab, with deep-rooted minority communities we will meet in their turn: the Jewish communities of the highlands (whose story runs from the Himyarite kings to Operation Magic Carpet), Ismaili Shia in the mountains and Hadhramaut, Sunni Shafi'i Muslims dominant in the south and along the coast, and the South Arabian linguistic fossils — the Mehri, Soqotri, and Hobyot languages of the Mahra and Socotra, closer to the tongues of ancient Arabia than to modern Arabic, preserving echoes of the world before Islam.

## 2. The First Yemenis: From the Stone Age to the Incense Roads

Archaeology in Yemen has been piecemeal — war, remoteness, and politics have limited excavation — but the picture is clear enough in outline.

**Palaeolithic hunters** left stone tool scatters across the Tihamah and the highland fringes. The earliest secure evidence of settled life grows denser in the **Neolithic period** (roughly 4000–3000 BCE), when domesticated wheat, barley, sheep, goats, and cattle appear — crops and animals that arrived from the Fertile Crescent to the north, carried by peoples who may have been speakers of the ancestral South Arabian languages.

The **Bronze Age** (3rd–early 1st millennium BCE) leaves more substantial traces: walled towns, irrigation works, and two distinctive cultures known to archaeologists — **Sabir**, in the Tihamah near present-day Aden, a cosmopolitan coastal settlement with links to Africa and India; and **Ma'layba**, a highland culture of the Jawf. Permanent communities farmed the wadis, herded in the steppe, and began doing something that would define Yemen forever: **trade**.

Arabia produced the most coveted luxury substances of the ancient world: **frankincense** (a resin burned in temples from Rome to Persia) and **myrrh** (used in medicine, cosmetics, and embalming). These grew almost exclusively in southern Arabia — in the hills of Hadhramaut and Dhofar — and Yemen sat at the northern end of the harvest zone, at the start of the camel-caravan roads that crossed a thousand miles of desert to reach the civilizations of Mesopotamia, the Levant, and Egypt.

Think of what this meant. Before oil, there was incense. Every temple in Babylon, every altar in Jerusalem, every funerary rite in Egypt consumed Arabian aromatics. The price was astronomical — Pliny the Elder famously complained that the Roman Empire spent enormous sums each year on the stuff. The caravan trade needed organization: wells, waystations, armed escorts, credit, weights and measures. Out of that necessity, around the late 2nd millennium BCE, the first great Yemeni states crystallized.

## 3. The Six Kingdoms: Saba and Its Rivals

Between roughly 1000 BCE and 300 CE, South Arabia was divided among several literate, urban, temple-centered kingdoms. They wrote in an alphabet called **Musnad** — a graceful, monumental script, ancestor of Ethiopia's Ge'ez alphabet — and they left tens of thousands of inscriptions, which is why we know their history in their own words, not just from outsiders.

**Saba (Sheba)** — the greatest of them, centered on Ma'rib and Sirwah in the Wadi Dhana. Its early rulers bore the title **mukarrib** — a religious-political office, something between high priest and king — before the title "king" (malik) became standard. Saba's wealth was the Ma'rib irrigation system and its grip on the northern incense routes. Its most famous documented king is **Karib'il Watar** (early 6th century BCE), whose long inscriptions boast of eight military campaigns against rebellious allies and rivals — including the destruction of the small kingdom of Awsan — and give us our earliest detailed war chronicle from Arabia.

Saba is also, of course, the kingdom of the **Queen of Sheba**. The Hebrew Bible tells of her visit to Solomon; the Qur'an tells of Bilqis and the hoopoe. Historians cannot confirm a historical queen or the visit, and this document makes no claim either way — but the legend itself is a historical fact of enormous consequence, binding Yemen into the sacred geography of three faiths.

**Ma'in** — northwest of Saba, a federation of city-states with a merchant aristocracy, famous for its contracts and treaties. Ma'inite trading colonies reached as far as Mesopotamia; the Bible's "Minaeans" carried incense to the Levant.

**Qataban** — centered on the wadi and city of **Timna**, with the handsome temple-palace complexes of the Bar'an complex near Ma'rib and Hajar bin Humeid. Qataban controlled the wadi routes south.

**Hadhramaut** — the eastern kingdom, centered on **Shabwa**, the great emporium where incense from the Dhofar groves was gathered, taxed, and shipped. The temple of Sayin at Shabwa was one of the richest shrines in Arabia.

**Awsan** — a smaller kingdom northwest of Qataban, destroyed by Karib'il Watar of Saba in one of his recorded campaigns.

These kingdoms fought, allied, married, minted coins, and above all traded. They built temples with monumental architecture and maintained the caravanserais of the "Incense Road" running up the western edge of Arabia to Gaza and Damascus. South Arabian kings appear in Assyrian inscriptions of the 8th–7th centuries BCE as tribute-payers and trading partners of Nineveh.

## 4. The Ma'rib Dam: The Engineering That Made a Civilization

No artifact symbolizes ancient Yemen like the **Dam of Ma'rib**. The Wadi Dhana is a seasonal torrent; the dam — first built perhaps as early as the 8th century BCE and rebuilt and enlarged repeatedly — converted its floods into a reservoir and fed two great sluice-canal systems (one to the north, one to the south) irrigating some tens of thousands of hectares of date groves and fields. Later Arab geographers describe a stone-and-earth structure over 100 metres long and in places 15–20 metres high, with bronze-sheathed sluice gates and a spillway.

The dam was less a single structure than a permanent civic religion: it required constant dredging, rebuilding, and collective labor — the famous inscriptions of the Himyarite kings boast of repairs after breaches. When the Qur'an later tells the story of the people of Saba and "the flood of the dam" (sayl al-'arim), it is preserving the folk memory of a real and recurrent catastrophe.

## 5. Himyar: The Last Great Kingdom, and Its Religious Revolution

Around 275 CE, **Himyar** — a kingdom based in the highlands southwest of Saba (capital Zafar) — absorbed Saba, Qataban, and Hadhramaut, unifying most of Yemen for the first time. Himyarite kings took the grand title **"King of Saba and dhu-Raydan and Hadhramaut and Yamnat"** — a claim to rule all of South Arabia.

Two revolutions mark the Himyarite age, and both changed world history.

**The first was religious.** From at least the 4th century, Himyar's ruling class adopted **Judaism** — the only state outside the Mediterranean world to do so at scale. Kings bore the title "Lord of the Jews and the Christians"; Hebrew appears in inscriptions; a Himyarite king composed verse in biblical Hebrew. Why Judaism? No outsider fully knows — trade connections, theological independence from both Christian Aksum and Zoroastrian Persia, and a desire for a monotheism that was neither Roman nor Persian all plausibly played a part. What is certain is the consequence: Himyar became the homeland of one of the world's oldest Jewish communities, whose descendants would live in Yemen for another fifteen centuries — through the great **Mawza exile** of 1679, when the Zaydi imam expelled the Jews to the hot coastal town of Mawza and many died on the march, and the era of the **Orphans' Decree** (forced conversion of Jewish orphans), until the twentieth century's airlifts. Their story is one of the oldest continuous threads in this whole document.

**The second revolution was imperial intervention.** Christianity was spreading across the Red Sea in the Kingdom of Aksum (Ethiopia), and Himyar's persecution of Christians — culminating in the massacre of the community at Najran around 523, commemorated in Christian martyr literature — gave Aksum's kings their casus belli. In **525 CE**, an Aksumite army under Kaleb invaded, killed the Jewish Himyarite king Yusuf As'ar Yath'ar (Dhu Nuwas in Muslim tradition), and installed a viceroy. Yemen became an Ethiopian satellite — and Ethiopia was a client of Byzantium. Yemen was now, for the first time, a front line in a global Cold War: Byzantium and its Ethiopian proxy on one shore of the Red Sea, Sasanian Persia on the other.

**Abraha**, the most famous of the Aksumite viceroys (r. c. 535–565), ruled in splendor from Sana'a and Taiz. Christian tradition credits him with a campaign against Mecca — the expedition remembered in the Qur'an as the **Year of the Elephant** (traditionally 570, the year of the Prophet Muhammad's birth), in which the invading force's war elephant allegedly refused to advance on the Kaaba and the army was destroyed by birds hurling stones. Historians treat the supernatural details as faith narrative; some also question whether the campaign happened at all, or whether Abraha targeted Mecca. What is archaeologically real is the final collapse of the Ma'rib Dam in the **570s** — the great flood that ended the irrigation system and scattered the Sabaean population. Arab historians preserved the memory in proverbs: "The Ma'rib dam did that to them" — meaning, their quarrels over maintenance destroyed their own prosperity. It is the first great Yemeni parable of self-inflicted ruin, and it will not be the last.

## 6. Persians, Byzantines, and the Coming of Islam

In about **570–578 CE**, the Sasanian Persian Empire — by treaty and intrigue with the disaffected Yemeni elite — expelled the Ethiopians and made Yemen a Persian satrapy. The last Persian governor, **Badhan**, was still in office when, around 628–631 CE, envoys from the new Muslim state at Medina arrived. Badhan accepted Islam; Yemen entered the community of the early caliphate largely by negotiation — one of the great civilizational pivots achieved without a major conquest.

What followed was a millennium of eclipse at the center but continuity at home. The incense trade had already declined (sea routes around Arabia were replacing the camel caravans), and Islam's sacred geography centered on Mecca, Medina, Jerusalem, Baghdad, and Cairo. Yemen became a **periphery of empires** — a producer of scholars, soldiers, and saints rather than of kings. Yet it was never passive. From the earliest caliphates onward, Yemen's mountains incubated a remarkable series of dissident and reforming movements.

# PART TWO — THE ISLAMIC MILLENNIUM

## 7. The First Islamic Centuries: Kharijites, Shi'a, and the Zaydi Revolution

Under the Rashidun, Umayyad, and early Abbasid caliphates, Yemen was ruled from afar — often through hereditary Arab governor-dynasties (the Ziyadids of Zabid in the Tihamah, the Najahids, the Yu'firids) and mountain strongmen. The old Himyarite aristocracy converted to Islam and merged into the new order.

Three streams of Islamic dissent matter for Yemen's future:

**The Kharijites**, who held parts of South Arabia and occasionally seized Sana'a, teaching that any pious Muslim could lead the community — a radical egalitarianism that repeatedly challenged caliphal authority.

**The Ismaili (Sevener) Shi'a da'wa** — the missionary movement of the Fatimid caliphs of Egypt, which won converts in Yemen from the 9th century and would briefly rule the whole country.

**And the one that built a state that lasted eleven hundred years: Zaydism.** Zaydism is a branch of Shi'a Islam holding that the rightful imam must be a learned descendant of the Prophet who rises against unjust rule. In **897 CE**, a Persian Alid scholar, **al-Hadi ila'l-Haqq Yahya**, answered appeals from the tribes of the Yemeni highlands and founded a theocratic imamate in the far north — in the region called Sa'da, which should be remembered carefully, because in the 21st century that small province would give its name to a war. The Zaydi imamate was a genuine state: it collected taxes, ran courts, appointed governors, and fought — against the Sunni lowlands, against Fatimid missionaries, against Egypt, and among its own competing imamic lines. The highland Zaydi heartland, and the tension between its theocratic tradition and the Sunni majority around it, is one of the deepest continuities in all of Yemeni history.

## 8. The Age of Queens and Sultans (10th–15th Centuries)

In 1047, the **Sulayhid dynasty** — Ismaili Shi'a — conquered much of Yemen under the brilliant **Queen Arwa bint Ahmad**, who ruled in her own right for decades, restored mosques, endowed schools, and held her realm together by diplomacy and force of personality. She is still revered across sectarian lines — a Yemeni national icon nine centuries later. Sulayhid power faded after her death (1138), and Yemen fragmented: the **Zurayids** held Aden; the **Hamdanids** and the messianic **Mahdids** contested the highlands. The Sunni **Ayyubids** of Egypt invaded (1174) and their successors, the **Rasulid sultans** (1229–1454), built the most splendid medieval Yemeni state — ruling from Taiz, patronizing astronomy, medicine and architecture, minting beautiful coins, and running an Indian Ocean trade network that reached East Africa, India, and China. The Chinese admiral **Zheng He** visited Aden in 1419; Rasulid ambassadors returned the journey. The Rasulids' fall in 1454 opened the way to the **Tahirids** (1454–1517), the last indigenous pre-Ottoman dynasty.

## 9. The Portuguese and the Ottomans Arrive

In **1507**, the Portuguese — newly masters of the Indian Ocean under Afonso de Albuquerque — seized Socotra, burned the city of Qishn in Hadhramaut, and tried to take Aden itself; the Adenis repelled them. Portugal wanted the spice trade; Yemen was in the way. Within a decade, the Mamluk sultanate of Egypt sent fleets to drive the Portuguese back, and in 1517–1518 the Ottoman Turks absorbed both Egypt and, nominally, Yemen.

The **first Ottoman occupation (1538–1635)** was brutal and extractive — the governor Hadim Suleiman Pasha's campaigns were remembered for their violence — and the Zaydi imamate in the mountains led the resistance. Ottoman power collapsed in the 1630s.

## 10. The Qasimi Imamate and the Coffee Empire (1635–1872)

What replaced the Ottomans was a genuinely Yemeni state: the **Qasimi imamate** (1597–1872), founded by the imam **al-Mansur al-Qasim** and consolidated by his son and successor **al-Mu'ayyad Muhammad**. The Qasimis reunified the highlands and coasts, drove the last foreigners out, and presided over Yemen's last age of global economic importance — the **coffee boom**.

Coffee — the word itself derives from the Yemeni port of **Mocha (al-Mukha)** — was Yemen's great 17th-century monopoly. The beans were grown on terraced mountain slopes; the trade was taxed at Mocha; Dutch, French, and English East India companies anchored offshore and paid cash. For a century, nearly every cup of coffee drunk in Europe passed through Yemeni hands. Imam **al-Mu'ayyad Muhammad** and his successors enforced the monopoly fiercely — forbidding the export of viable seed on pain of death (the Dutch eventually smuggled seedlings out, breaking the monopoly by planting Java in the 1690s–1700s).

The Qasimi state was also theocratic and increasingly insular — sumptuary laws regulating dress, hostility to foreign customs, and a standing tension between the imam's religious authority and tribal autonomy. When the coffee trade migrated away (to Java, then to the Caribbean) and the Mocha trade declined, Yemen's economy hollowed out. By the 18th century, the imamate was a proud, isolated, impoverished mountain kingdom — while a new power was moving into its backyard.

# PART THREE — EMPIRES, COLONIES, AND TWO YEMENS

## 11. The British Take Aden (1839)

On **19 January 1839**, a British East India Company force stormed **Aden**, a decaying but superbly positioned port at the foot of a volcano, to use as a coaling station on the steamship route to India. The sultan of the **Abdali** principality (Lahej) was driven out; his successors signed "protection" treaties that made them, one by one, British clients. The logic was imperial and frank: Aden guarded the route to India and, after the Suez Canal opened in 1869, became one of the most valuable pieces of real estate in the entire British Empire — a fortress colony with a world-class coaling and, later, oil-bunkering harbor.

Aden boomed as a free port: Jews from Yemen and India, Arabs, Somalis, Indians, Parsees, and Europeans made it the busiest port between Suez and Bombay. The hinterland, meanwhile, remained what it had always been: a patchwork of sultanates (the Qu'aiti and Kathiri sultanates of Hadhramaut, the Mahra sultanate, the Federation of South Arabia that Britain assembled in the 1950s–60s) — proud, poor, and increasingly restless.

## 12. The Ottomans Return (1872–1918)

The Qasimi imamate spent the 19th century decaying into civil wars among rival imams. The opportunity was not lost on Istanbul, which re-occupied the highlands and coasts in **1872** and organized the **Vilayet of Yemen**. Ottoman Yemen was run by a vali (governor) in Sana'a, a bureaucracy, garrisons in every town — and it was bitterly resented. The Zaydi tribes of the north rose repeatedly in what the Ottomans called the "Yemen problem." By 1911, the great-power chessboard gave the tribes their opening: with Italy attacking Ottoman Libya, the Imam **Yahya Muhammad Hamid al-Din** — who had taken the imamate in 1904 and held out through the Ottoman siege of his mountain capital — won the **Treaty of Da'an** (1911), securing autonomy in the Zaydi highlands.

## 13. Independence and the Kingdom of Yemen (1918–1962)

When the Ottoman Empire collapsed in World War I, Imam Yahya declared an independent **Mutawakkilite Kingdom of Yemen** (1926), with himself as king and imam in one — God's vicar and head of state, a theocratic monarchy in direct succession from the Qasimis. Yahya spent the 1920s and 1930s extending his writ by treaty and conquest over the Tihamah, Hadhramaut, and Mahra — and in **1934** went to war with the new Saudi state over the provinces of Asir, Najran, and Jizan. The Saudis won; the **Treaty of Taif** fixed the border and ceded those lands to Riyadh — a territorial wound that still shapes Yemeni-Saudi relations.

The kingdom was medieval in structure: no modern army, no industry, near-total isolation. Yahya was assassinated in a **1948 coup** led by the Free Yemeni Movement — reformers who wanted a constitutional monarchy. The coup — the al-Wazir plot, named for its leader Abdullah al-Wazir — failed; his son **Ahmad bin Yahya** took the throne and ruled as a paranoid absolutist until his death in September 1962.

## 14. The South: Federation, Emergency, and the PDRY (1950s–1967)

British Aden, meanwhile, had become a hotbed of Arab nationalism — "Little Moscow" was its nickname — and the surrounding protectorates were organized into the Federation of South Arabia. Nationalist guerrillas (the NLF, National Liberation Front, and its rival FLOSY) launched the **Aden Emergency (1963–1967)**: bombings, assassinations, riots in the Crater district. Britain, exhausted and unwilling to fight a second Aden war, withdrew on **30 November 1967**. The NLF took power and proclaimed the **People's Democratic Republic of Yemen (PDRY)** — South Yemen — the Arab world's only Marxist state.

## 15. Two States, One Country (1967–1990)

**South Yemen (PDRY):** radical, secular, Soviet-armed. Land reform, mass literacy campaigns, women's emancipation decrees — and one-party rule. Its first president was **Qahtan al-Sha'abi**; he was ousted in 1969 by the more radical faction of **Salim Rubai Ali** (Salmin), who fell to a Soviet-backed "Corrective Step" in 1978 that elevated **Abdul Fattah Ismail**. South Yemen closed the Bab al-Mandab to Israeli-bound shipping during the **October 1973 war** — its one hour of global strategic consequence — and hosted the largest Soviet naval base outside the Warsaw Pact at Aden. But its politics were a knife-fight: in **January 1986** the party split and Aden saw a week of urban warfare — the South Yemeni civil war — that killed thousands (estimates around 10,000) and exiled **Ali Nasser Muhammad** to the North. The survivors, led by **Haidar al-Attas**, never recovered; with the USSR collapsing, unification talks with the North — under way since 1972 — finally succeeded.

**North Yemen (Yemen Arab Republic, 1962–1990):** the North's story was a civil war. When Imam Ahmad died in September 1962, army officers led by **Abdullah al-Sallal** overthrew his son **al-Badr** and proclaimed a republic. Al-Badr escaped to the mountains; the royalist tribes rose. What followed — the **North Yemen Civil War (1962–1970)** — became a Cold War proxy in miniature: **Egypt's Nasser sent 70,000 troops** and aircraft to the republicans; **Saudi Arabia and Jordan** backed the imamate; British special forces advised the royalists. Sana'a was besieged, villages were bombed, and perhaps 100,000–200,000 people died before the exhausted republicans, under President **Abdul Rahman al-Iryani**, won a grudging peace in 1970.

The republic that emerged was a tribal-military oligarchy of remarkable fragility: President **Ibrahim al-Hamdi** (assassinated 1977), his successor **Ahmad al-Ghashmi** (assassinated 1978), and then a young officer from the Hashid tribal confederation: **Ali Abdullah Saleh**, president from July 1978, age 36. Saleh survived everything — border wars with the South (1972, 1979, 1986), assassinations, coups — by the arts of patronage: balancing the great tribal confederations (**Hashid** under the al-Ahmar sheikhs, **Bakil** to the west), buying off the Islamist **Islah** party, and keeping the army a patchwork of personal loyalties. His regime motto might as well have been his reported governing principle: dancing on the heads of snakes.

**Unification** came on **22 May 1990** — partly idealism, mostly arithmetic: the North had the people and oil; the South had the institutions and the ports. It was to be a partnership of equals. It was not. When the South tried to secede in **1994** after losing the power struggle, Saleh's northern army crushed the secession in a two-month civil war; the South was occupied, its army dissolved, its assets stripped — a wound that never closed and that, thirty years later, would reopen as the Southern Transitional Council.

## 16. The Saleh System (1990–2011)

Saleh's unified Yemen ran on oil and favors. Oil had been discovered in **Marib in 1984** and came onstream in 1986–88; by the 2000s petroleum supplied the bulk of state revenue and most of the hard currency. The state, in turn, was the prize: a 2011 study found that **ten families and business groups connected to Saleh controlled over 80% of imports, manufacturing, telecommunications, banking, and transport** — and the largest of these, the **Hayel Saeed Anam (HSA) Group**, would become, in the war years, the single biggest food provider in the country. Keep this fact in a safe place; we will return to it when we follow the food money.

Qat — the mildly narcotic leaf chewed daily by most Yemeni men — consumed somewhere around a third of household income and, worse, a huge share of the nation's groundwater, an addiction economics no government dared touch.

Under Saleh, Yemen was the West's indispensable counterterrorism partner after 2000, when suicide bombers in Aden harbor blew a hole in the USS **Cole**, killing 17 American sailors (the attack was al-Qaeda's work; a USS Cole bombing suspect, Nasser al-Baha/al-Shiba, would resurface decades later commanding a southern militia unit — a small emblem of how the war dissolved every old category). Saleh took Washington's money and drone strikes, crushed and co-opted al-Qaeda in turn, and held elections — winning each with cartoonish 90-plus percent.

But in the highlands of **Sa'da province**, a new movement was growing: the **Believing Youth**, a Zaydi revivalist network led by a charismatic cleric, **Hussein Badreddin al-Houthi**. Saleh saw a threat; the Houthis saw persecution. Between 2004 and 2010, the government fought **six Sa'dah wars**; Hussein al-Houthi was killed in 2004; his brother **Abdul-Malik** took up the cause; the wars radicalized the movement and taught it to fight.

Then came 2011. The Arab Spring reached Yemen in force; Saleh's own tribal and military allies peeled away after the **March 18, 2011 massacre** of protesters in Sana'a. The **GCC Initiative** choreographed his exit: immunity from prosecution in exchange for stepping down, with elections anointing his vice president, **Abd Rabbuh Mansur Hadi**, as the consensus candidate in February 2012. A National Dialogue Conference (2013–2014) drafted a new federal map for Yemen — six regions — that pleased almost no one.

# PART FOUR — THE WAR (2014–2026)

## 17. How the Houthis Took Sana'a (2014–2015)

The immediate trigger was an economic one — remember this, because economics is the true grammar of this war. In July 2014, Hadi's government, under IMF tutelage, cut fuel subsidies overnight, roughly doubling fuel prices; protests exploded; Hadi reshuffled his cabinet but the damage was done. The Houthis, well armed and battle-hardened from the Sa'dah wars, marched south with a populist message — against corruption, against the subsidy cut, against al-Qaeda — and with quiet encouragement from forces within Saleh's old network (Saleh himself still commanded deep loyalty in the army).

On **21 September 2014**, the Houthis entered Sana'a. In January 2015, besieging the presidential palace, they forced a power-sharing deal; by February they had placed Hadi under house arrest. Hadi escaped to Aden, then to Riyadh. On **26 March 2015**, the day after the Houthis — now allied with Saleh's forces — advanced on Aden itself, a coalition led by **Saudi Arabia** (with the UAE as its operational partner) launched **Operation Decisive Storm**: airstrikes to restore Hadi and roll back what Riyadh called an Iranian proxy on its southern border.

## 18. Why Each Actor Fought (the motive file, stated without endorsement)

Understanding the dead requires understanding the calculators.

**Saudi Arabia:** Defense Minister **Mohammed bin Salman (MBS)**, 29 years old, was the war's chief architect. The stated rationale: Iran was building a "Hezbollah on the southern border," and Houthi missiles could one day hold Saudi cities hostage. The deeper drivers: prestige and succession politics for the young prince; post-2011 counter-revolution — restoring order on the Arabian Peninsula after the Arab Spring terrified every monarchy; and the doctrine, held sincerely in Riyadh, that allowing one Iranian-aligned militia to win would invite encirclement (Iran's "four capitals" rhetoric). Cost was never the obstacle: the kingdom was earning the money back in weapons purchases' strategic leverage and, until 2019, in American and British political cover.

**UAE:** Crown Prince **Mohammed bin Zayed (MBZ)** wanted different things: to crush political Islam (the Yemeni Islah party, branch of the Muslim Brotherhood, was his obsession — hence the assassination program later documented against Islah figures); to build a string of ports and bases across the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden (Socotra, Perim, Assab in Eritrea, Berbera in Somaliland, Aden); and to do it with other people's soldiers — Sudanese, Eritrean, Colombian, Chadian, later Colombian again — so that Emirati casualties stayed near zero.

**United States:** Under **Obama**, initial support was reflexive Gulf-loyalty plus counterterrorism (al-Qaeda's most dangerous branch was in Yemen). Under **Trump**, it became arms-market economics and Iran-hawk politics — Secretary of State **Mike Pompeo** personally certified, against the evidence of his own department's reporting, that the coalition was reducing civilian casualties, and invoked an "emergency" to sell $8.1 billion in weapons past Congress. Defense Secretary **James Mattis** put the Pentagon's role plainly: "we help them plan what we call targeting." The US stopped mid-air refueling only in November 2018, after Senate pressure — and kept providing intelligence, training, and maintenance throughout. A British peer's description of the whole arrangement was not unfair: Washington treated the war as a production line subsidy with foreign-policy benefits.

**United Kingdom:** Commercial survival. **BAE Systems'** Typhoon production line and its Saudi service contracts (over £15 billion in Saudi revenue since 2015) made the war, in effect, industrial policy. Foreign Secretary **Jeremy Hunt** lobbied Germany to lift its embargo so the British and European arms industries wouldn't suffer. The Ministry of Defence office that keeps the Saudi air force flying — MODSAP, over a hundred strong — is a child of the same forty-year embrace.

**France:** Market share and Gulf commercial diplomacy — while its own military intelligence (the leaked **DRM "Yemen Papers,"** September 2018) told the Élysée exactly what French artillery, tanks, aircraft, and warships were doing in and around Yemen.

**Iran:** Cheap leverage. For the cost of some advisors, smuggled components, and dhows of fuel and weapons, Tehran bought a permanent thorn in Riyadh's side and, from 2023, a hand on the world's shipping jugular. The investment was trivial against the coalition's hundreds of billions.

**The Houthis:** Survival became self-financing. The war built them a state: the extraction economy (documented below at $5–6 billion a year) now dwarfs anything peace would offer; war also supplies their theology of martyrdom, their identity, and — after 2023 — their global relevance.

## 19. The Air War and What It Killed

The coalition's air campaign is one of the most documented in modern warfare, and the documentation is damning in both directions of responsibility.

**Scale:** The independent **Yemen Data Project** logged **25,054 coalition air raids through March 2022**. Only 32% of identifiable targets were military; **28% were clearly civilian** — homes, markets, schools, hospitals, weddings, funerals, fishing boats, water systems, food stores. The Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project (ACLED) attributes roughly **67% of all directly-targeted civilian fatalities to the coalition** — the single largest killer of civilians in the war, ahead of the Houthis' shelling and mines.

**Named strikes, traced weapons:**
- **Dahyan, 9 August 2018 — the school bus.** Fifty-one killed, forty of them children. CNN traced the bomb to a **Lockheed Martin laser-guided Mk 82**; Bellingcat traced the fin markings to a 1,000-bomb shipment the US State Department approved in 2015. Riyadh called it a "legitimate military operation."
- **Great Hall funeral, Sana'a, 8 October 2016 — 155 killed** by a US-supplied Mk 84-series bomb. Obama restricted precision munitions over it; **Rex Tillerson lifted the restriction in March 2017**.
- **The Hajjah wedding, April 2018:** at least 21 killed, nearly 60 children among the wounded, by a US-made bomb.
- **Mastaba market, 15 March 2016 — 97 civilians killed, 25 children** — a General Dynamics 2,000-pound bomb with a Boeing JDAM kit. Saudi Arabia commissioned 59,000 GBU-12 Paveways from Raytheon the following year.
- **Kouhaza community college, Sana'a, 8 & 10 January 2016** — a **UK-made Hakim A series precision-guided bomb built by GEC-Marconi Dynamics (later BAE)**, most likely released by a UAE Mirage 2000-9 or F-16E/F.
- **Dhamar detention-center strike, 31 August 2019 — roughly 100 detainees killed.** The Houthis had imprisoned critics there; the coalition bombed it anyway, and spokesman Turki al-Maliki defended the strike. Both facts belong on the same page.
- The forensic dossier "**Day of Judgment**" (Mwatana, the University Network for Human Rights, PAX, 2019): 27 apparently unlawful coalition attacks, 203 civilians killed, 749 injured; US-made weapons in 25 of 27 cases, UK-made in five.

**Cluster munitions:** at least **23 coalition attacks (2015–2017) using seven munition types from three countries** — US CBU-105s (Textron; the US suspended transfers in May 2016 and Textron, the last US cluster-maker, ended production that August), US CBU-87s and CBU-58s, **British BL755s** (the first recorded British cluster use since the ban treaty took effect; Riyadh promised to stop in December 2016), Brazilian ASTROS II rockets, US M26 rockets. Coalition spokesman Ahmed al-Asiri admitted the CBU-105 use on CNN, then claimed the ban "isn't correct." (A decade earlier, on **17 December 2009**, US Tomahawk cruise missiles carrying cluster munitions had struck the hamlet of **al-Majalah** in Abyan, killing 55 people including 41 civilians — the war's first documented cluster use, never compensated, never officially acknowledged in detail.)

**The accountability sham:** The coalition's own Joint Incidents Assessment Team recommended discipline or further investigation in **2 of about 75 public reports**. Human Rights Watch called the pattern what it was: a whitewash.

**Hospitals and healers:** The war on medical care was methodical: Physicians for Human Rights and Mwatana documented **120 attacks on health facilities and personnel between March 2015 and December 2018 — at least 96 civilians and health workers killed (10 of them children), 230 wounded**, with 35 facilities struck from the air and Taiz alone recording 67 incidents. After the **Abs hospital strike of 15 August 2016** — 19 dead including newborns, in a hospital whose coordinates Médecins Sans Frontières had shared with the coalition twelve times — MSF evacuated six hospitals. Six documented cases involved Houthi military use of facilities, compromising their protection: the crime ledger belongs to both sides.

**The other side's ledger:** The Houthis fired 430+ ballistic missiles and 851 drones into Saudi Arabia by late 2021 (killing 59 Saudi civilians), shelled Taiz city for years — one of the longest continuous sieges anywhere in the world — laid **over a million landmines** (Yemen is likely the most-mined country since WWII; more than 5,500 people, many of them children and shepherds, have been killed by mines and IEDs), recruited children by the thousand (a senior Houthi official admitted 18,000; UN verification confirmed 1,940 recruited 2013–18 and nearly 2,000 Houthi-recruited children killed in combat in just 17 months of 2020–21), and ran a torture archipelago: hanging prisoners from ceilings, pouring acid on bodies, extracting nails — documented by the Associated Press (2018) and the NGO Mwatana, including the 2023 death in custody of 28-year-old Ezzedine al-Humaigani. In November 2025, Houthi courts sentenced 17 people to death for alleged espionage, part of a sweep that has detained **73 UN staff since 2021** and gutted the aid operation (see §22).

## 20. The 377,000 — How the Dying Actually Worked

In November 2021, a UNDP-commissioned study modeled Yemen against a no-war counterfactual and projected that by year's end the war would have caused **377,000 deaths — 60% of them "indirect," meaning no bullet or bomb was involved** — and that **roughly 70% of the total, 259,000, were children under five**. In 2021, a Yemeni child under five died every nine minutes. The model's direct component (~150,000) comes from ACLED's incident data; its indirect component from 91 household mortality surveys run through Bayesian models — with the researchers' own caveat that more than half the surveys excluded insecure areas, so the figure is a floor, not a ceiling. The same model's forward projection is the war's most chilling number: on the conflict's then-trajectory, **1.3 million people would die by 2030, the majority of them children under five**. This is why the true toll can never be audited: the statisticians were locked out of the graveyard.

**The cholera.** Between 2016 and 2022 Yemen suffered the largest cholera outbreak ever recorded — over **2.5 million suspected cases**, 84–93% of all the world's cholera cases in 2017 and 2019, the majority of them children. The mechanism was man-made twice over: in September 2016 President Hadi moved the Central Bank from Sana'a to Aden to deny the Houthis state finances — and **civil-servant salaries, including those of some 30,000 health workers, stopped nationwide**. Sanitation workers in Sana'a, unpaid, went on strike; garbage washed into the water supply; the sewer system failed ten days before the April 2017 surge. Genomic tracing placed the strain in East Africa, carried in by migrants across a border the war had destroyed. And the vaccine response failed: a one-million-dose emergency plan was retracted in June 2017; the first vaccination campaign arrived **sixteen months and 1.2 million cases late**. A peer-reviewed post-mortem calls it "a historic example of the failure to control the spread of cholera," blaming parties and donors alike.

**The famine that was never declared.** By May 2021, five million Yemenis were one step from starvation and sixteen million were hungry. The warnings had been public and dire for years: on 23 October 2018 the UN's humanitarian chief, Mark Lowcock, told the Security Council there was "a clear and present danger of an imminent and great big famine engulfing Yemen — much bigger than anything that any professional in this field has seen during their working lives," formally invoking the Council's own Resolution 2417 on starvation in war. And yet no famine was ever formally declared. Not because it wasn't happening: because famine declaration requires mortality data that could not exist in a country where access had collapsed. The warning machinery did its job and was then quietly disarmed at the declaration layer: FEWS NET (the US famine-early-warning system) published explicit famine-risk alerts throughout 2018 — and was itself **shut down by US funding cuts in February 2025**, two decades into its successor systems' struggles. But the **IPC** — the international body whose word formally declares famine — saw its November 2018 analysis delayed past the UN's own statements, its Famine Review Committee activated and then sidelined, and a peer-reviewed study later found "truncated" distributions in 158 of 333 districts (crisis populations but mathematically zero famine cases) alongside what it called "a fear of some of the major donors — including Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, who are also major actors in the conflict," who funded 40% of the response. Inside the process, participants described a consensus driven by "the loudest voice in the room" — the agencies holding the data — while the Sana'a health ministry had replaced experienced statisticians with political appointees, injecting an element of political control over the analysis. The UN Resident Coordinator, Lise Grande, conceded "we will almost certainly never know concretely what is going on with the mortality indicator." The record must also preserve the dissent: **Médecins Sans Frontières publicly argued there was no quality data to declare an imminent famine** — the counter-position, kept honestly on file.

**The suppression apparatus, in full:**
- In 2016 Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon listed the coalition in his annual report on children and armed conflict (which had verified 785 children killed and 1,168 wounded, attributing 60% of the child casualties to coalition airstrikes) — and delisted it within days after Riyadh threatened to withdraw "hundreds of millions of dollars" (including for Palestinian refugees), calling it one of the most painful decisions of his tenure. His successor António Guterres relisted the coalition in 2017 inside a newly invented intermediate category, dropped it from the schools-and-hospitals list in 2018 despite 19 documented attacks, and in June 2020 removed it entirely — a decision the US Secretary of State **Mike Pompeo backed** — on a day the coalition killed 13 civilians including four children.
- In 2018 the UN accepted Saudi Arabia's **$930 million** humanitarian contribution with a leaked "Visibility Plan" tying aid to "the amount of beneficial publicity given to Saudi Arabia" (the kingdom and the UAE together funded ~40% of the response). The conditionality ran deeper than publicity: in 2015, Riyadh's $244 million for the emergency appeal sat undistributed for two months while its aid agency demanded the money be spent only outside Houthi areas.
- Even hardware became leverage: the coalition **bombed Hodeidah's cargo cranes in 2015**; the World Food Programme's US-funded replacements reached Dubai in early 2017 and the coalition **refused for months to let them in**, arguing they would benefit the Houthis.
- In 2020 the UN's own human-rights investigators (the Group of Eminent Experts) were abolished at the Human Rights Council by a 21–18–7 vote after a documented Saudi-Emirati lobbying campaign — the first time such a mandate was ever killed.

**The blockade's arithmetic.** Yemen imports ~90% of its food. The coalition's naval and air blockade — maintained, tightened, and periodically choked — plus the Houthi counter-measures on fuel, turned imports into a weapon. After a Houthi missile hit Riyadh airport in November 2017, the coalition closed *all* Yemeni ports for weeks; AP/Save the Children reported **50,000 children dead of starvation and disease in 2017 alone** — 130 a day at the peak. (A later Save the Children claim of 85,000 children dead across the war's first three years circulates widely but carries a "dubious" flag in the standard reference record; the UNDP's 259,000 under-five total is the defensible anchor.) The UN Panel of Experts formally concluded both sides used fuel regulation "as a tool of economic warfare": the Houthis, having agreed to deposit fuel taxes in a special account to pay salaries, withdrew **45 billion rials** and stopped sharing bank statements; the government countered with Decrees 49 and 75 restricting imports; fuel oil in Hodeidah quintupled in price while tankers carrying half a million tonnes idled offshore under coalition holds. Every fuel shortage cascaded into water pumping, hospital generators, and food transport — the mortar and pestle that ground the 259,000.

The inspection regime itself became part of the machine. The UN Verification and Inspection Mechanism (UNVIM) — established in 2016 at the government's request, headquartered in Djibouti, coalition-funded — inspects only the three northern ports (Hodeidah, Salif, Ras Isa), leaving Aden's flows unexamined; it runs on $11 million a year, took over a year to stand up after Saudi Arabia refused entry to its first coordinator, and by the British government's own finding serviced over 500 unsearched ship calls at Hodeidah across eight months of 2024. Its record deserves the full arc: a UN Office on Drugs and Crime study found little evidence of weapons smuggling through the Red Sea between 2015 and 2022 — the sea lane opened only when the truce loosened restrictions and the Gaza war raised the stakes, after which direct Iran-to-Hodeidah cargo ships, Horn-of-Africa transshipment, and floating IRGC support vessels carried the flow (see §25). Every tanker waiting for clearance also paid demurrage — a quiet private tax on the siege.

The NGO consortium Mwatana and Global Rights Compliance put the verdict on record for both sides: the coalition **and** the Houthis "**used starvation as a method of warfare**" — a war crime.

## 21. The Money Map I — The War Chests and the Arms Ledger

**Who paid for the war, and who got paid.**

The United States offered Saudi Arabia over $138 billion in arms deals across 2009–2019, with **four companies — Raytheon, Lockheed Martin, Boeing, General Dynamics — covering more than 90% of it**. The flagship was Trump's 2017 Riyadh package: $110 billion immediate, $350 billion over a decade. When Congress balked at precision-guided bombs, **Pompeo declared a national emergency (24 May 2019) to force through $8.1 billion**; the State Department's own inspector general later found **$11.2 billion in approvals kept below congressional notification thresholds** and concluded the department "did not fully assess risks to reduce civilian casualties." Trump vetoed every congressional resolution to end the war (three of them in July 2019, one in April). Raytheon's numbers tell the tale in one line: more than **120,000 bombs and bomb parts worth over $3 billion to the coalition 2015–20**, 6,120 Paveways authorized in the war's first year, **59,000 GBU-12 Paveways commissioned in 2019 alone** — and a stock price that climbed from about $108 to over $180 between 2015 and 2019. Lockheed Martin, the world's largest arms-maker by 2019 revenue ($53 billion), sold the kingdom 154 F-15s and 90 Apache attack helicopters over the longer relationship; Boeing and General Dynamics filled out the heavy armor — 420 M1 tanks, 400 Bradley fighting vehicles, some 600 M109 howitzers in 2016–18 deals alone.

The United Kingdom licensed **£7.1 billion** in arms to Saudi Arabia 2015–22 (£31 billion estimated real value), with **BAE Systems taking £15 billion+ in Saudi revenue**. The Court of Appeal ruled the licensing **unlawful** in June 2019; the government of Liz Truss resumed it thirteen months later, classifying mass-casualty strikes as "isolated incidents"; in 2023 the High Court rejected a new challenge — on closed evidence the public could not see. France delivered **€4.7 billion+ of war materiel to the coalition** (over €8 billion to both coalition states 2015–20): 48 and later **147 CAESAR howitzers**, Leclerc tanks, Mirage jets, a refueling tanker, and — per its own leaked military-intelligence note of September 2018 — **two French warships enforcing the blockade**. Nexter Munitions depends on Saudi Arabia for 55% of its business; the Belgian firm Mecar for 80%. Spain, Italy and Belgium kept selling (Spanish banks BBVA and Santander alone financed $5.2 billion of the exporting companies, 2015–19; EU states issued over 600 licences worth €15.8 billion to Riyadh in 2016 alone); Germany, Denmark, Finland, the Netherlands and Norway restricted sales after the Khashoggi murder.

And beneath it all, the older corruption: **BAE's £43 billion al-Yamamah programme** (1985–2007) generated up to **£6 billion in commissions, over £1 billion of it to Prince Bandar bin Sultan via Saudi embassy accounts at Riggs Bank in Washington**; the Serious Fraud Office's investigation was shut down in 2006 after **Tony Blair wrote a secret personal letter to the Attorney General** demanding it stop; BAE later paid a $400 million US fine for "intentionally failing" anti-bribery controls; and the National Audit Office's report on the programme remains, uniquely, withheld from publication. The MOD office that services the Saudi air force (MODSAP, over a hundred staff) still keeps the Typhoon fleet flying today.

**Logistics:** Saudi state shipping line **Bahri** moved the hardware: EU parliamentary questions document its vessels switching off AIS transponders, falsifying routes, and loading at night in European ports; in May 2019 French and Italian dockworkers blocked the Bahri Yanbu at Le Havre and Genoa (eight Caesar howitzers were aboard the French load; the ship left empty); the Spanish port of Bilbao shipped Expal/Maxam ammunition monthly; five Italian dockers of the Calp collective were charged with criminal conspiracy for their blockades. Meanwhile, classified US weapons transferred by Saudi Arabia and the UAE ended up in the hands of **al-Qaeda-linked militias** — the Giants Brigades, the Abu Abbas Brigade — per CNN's investigation, prompting a formal House Foreign Affairs Committee inquiry that went nowhere; and the Associated Press documented something worse: secret deals in which coalition partners paid al-Qaeda fighters to withdraw from contested areas, preserving AQAP as a fighting force throughout the war. (AQAP's own file in this war is long: the 2000 USS Cole; the 2011–12 conquest of Abyan and Shabwa towns; the quiet year it held Mukalla, Yemen's fifth city, in 2015–16; the secret-prison economy that ran alongside it.)

## 22. The Money Map II — The Houthi Extraction State

The blockade built the Houthis a fiscal empire. The UN Panel of Experts (2025) put fuel-import customs at **~$4 billion for 2022–24**, with total fuel-sector income including illegal fees at **1.34 trillion rials (~$5.5 billion)**; the Stolen Asset Recovery Initiative tracked **$789 million in port revenues**. A Yemeni Finance Ministry leak put 2023 revenue across 13 sectors at **3.39 trillion rials (~$6.4 billion)**; the six-month 2022 truce alone, when fuel flowed freely, filled Houthi coffers with an estimated 300 billion rials in fuel duties; four telecom 4G licenses alone raised **$1.25 billion**; roughly **70% of all Yemen's tax revenue** is collected in Houthi territory; the Sana'a Center estimates $1.8 billion a year in general levies — including retroactive taxes calculated back to a company's founding year.

The named machinery: the **Yemen Petroleum Company** (fuel monopoly; importers must hand over cargo to it); the **General Authority of Zakat** (centralizing religious taxes since 2018); the **Khums amendment** (April 2020) diverting 20% of natural-resource profits to "Hashemite" families; and a constellation of front companies sanctioned by the US Treasury — **Black Diamond Petroleum Derivatives, Star Plus Yemen, Tamco, Royal Plus** (selling IRGC-sourced oil and brokering Houthi–Iran–Russia payments for drone engines), **Al-Usaili** (IRGC-linked), **Gasoline Aman, Azzahra** ("laundering hundreds of millions of dollars"), **Yemen Elaph** (owner Abdullah Ahsan Abdullah Dabbash), and **Abbot Trading** (run by the Talea brothers). The January 2026 Treasury action added 21 more names — the Adeema Oil owner Waleed Baidhani, Iranian national Imran Asghar's Alsaa Petroleum, the Al-Ridhwan Exchange (holding accounts for the Houthi "Ministry of Defense," overseen by Mohammed Abdulsalam's aide Muhammad al-Talibi), the Rabya trading firm — while earlier rounds designated the **International Bank of Yemen** (April 2025) and the **Yemen Kuwait Bank** (January 2025), designated the movement SDGT (February 2024) and FTO (March 2025), and exposed eight Houthi-linked cryptocurrency wallets routing money toward drone manufacturers. Outside sits **Sa'id al-Jamal's** sanctioned oil-smuggling network — moving tens of millions through front exchanges such as Swaid and Sons and UAE-based Adoon General Trading — with evidence the Houthis resell Iranian oil and weapons onward to al-Shabaab and ISIS-Somalia (flagged: GNET-sourced, lead-grade). Their revenue also came from the aid system itself: a demanded **2% cut of the entire aid budget ($60–80 million)**, documented diversion of ~1,200 tonnes of food a month through seven Sana'a centers, 2,000 tons of food left to rot in Aslam — a documented starvation site where villagers ate boiled leaves — a $1.6 million warehouse seizure in 2025, and a **mahram rule** (requiring male chaperones for female aid workers) that gutted services to women. The WFP's partial suspension in June 2019 (850,000 people affected) failed to change the pattern. By January 2026, the World Food Programme had shut its northern operations entirely; the UN operates only in government areas while roughly 70% of humanitarian need sits in Houthi territory, and the group's own budget shows the strain: fighter pay four months in arrears, health-worker salaries suspended, Hodeidah customs dented by Israeli strikes.

## 23. The Money Map III — The Aden Side, and the Mercenary Wars

The internationally recognized government's own corruption is equally documented: the UN Panel accused the Aden central bank of facilitating the **embezzlement of $423 million of the $2 billion Saudi deposit** (made in January 2018, alarmed by events), with **HSA Group receiving $194.2 million** — nearly half — through letters of credit at preferential exchange rates; a leaked board memo accused governor Hafedh Mayad of squandering $975 million; no audit has ever been conducted since the bank's 2016 relocation. The prehistory is on file too: Aden's governor resigned in 2017 denouncing Prime Minister Ahmed bin Daghr's "huge camp of corruption"; the Emirati-backed STC seized Aden over the same grievance in January 2018 — with Emirati jets bombing Hadi's own presidential guard base to enable it — and Riyadh committed the $2 billion days later (with fresh banknotes printed in Russia). Today only **30–40% of customs duties due are actually collected** — the rest leaks to corruption and to the illegal tolls of armed checkpoints — and the visible books are rich: some **250 billion rials** from the sovereign ports and ~150 billion from the free-zone and container customs in the first eight months of 2025 alone, ~10 billion a month from the tax authority, billions more from a cigarette factory; the governor of Aden at one point halted revenue transfers to the central bank under STC pressure. The subsidy system leaks ~YR200 billion a year in under-priced fuel to well-connected beneficiaries; the December 2019 currency schism (Aden banning Sana'a-printed notes) created two rials and a vast arbitrage; and Riyadh keeps the government alive with structured transfusions — a $3 billion package on the Presidential Leadership Council's formation (of which only ~$1 billion was actually dispersed), then $1 billion, then $1.2 billion, then $422 million in fuel, and nearly **$3 billion budgeted for 2026 salaries** — because its $1.25 trillion giga-projects need a quiet southern border. The Saudi-funded **National Shield Forces** (7 brigades, 8,000 fighters) stand outside the Yemeni chain of command as the guarantor.

**The mercenary pipelines — the war's outsourced muscle.**

- **Sudan/Darfur:** the Saudis and UAE hired the **Rapid Support Forces (RSF)** — the Janjaweed successor commanded by **Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo ("Hemeti")** — paying **$480–530/month plus a $10,000 end-of-tour bonus** through the partly Saudi-owned **Faisal Islamic Bank of Sudan**; soldiers reported 20–40% of their units were children aged 14–17; the NYT found a 16-year-old veteran recruited at 14 ("families know that the only way their lives will change is if their sons join the war"). Up to 30,000 Sudanese rotated through Yemen's toughest fronts at the war's peak. The UN's investigators documented children trained in Saudi Arabia then deployed; the US State Department recorded officials recruiting Darfuri children; a US congressional letter and a ban on refueling followed. The pipeline's second life is the through-line of our time: the fighters came home (2019–20) with Saudi weapons and combat pay, the RSF metastasized from ~6,000 to over 100,000 men, and the same force committed the El Geneina ethnic cleansing, the Ardamata massacre (community tallies up to 2,000 dead), and the April 2025 Zam Zam camp massacre (500+ dead, girls as young as 13 raped). El Fasher fell on 26 October 2025 after an 18-month siege, under Hemeti's brother **Abdul Rahim Hamdan Dagalo** (EU-sanctioned November 2025; UNSC-sanctioned weeks later); the UN Fact-Finding Mission's September 2026 report found the atrocities carry "hallmarks of genocide" and that Emirati-linked entities "played a pivotal role" in recruiting, financing, transporting, and deploying the foreign contractors. The UAE's answer to the ICJ was jurisdictional; its answer to the world was a $500 million pledge.
- **The American hit squad:** in December 2015, a dozen US special-operations veterans — founded by Hungarian-Israeli **Abraham Golan**, run with ex-SEAL **Isaac Gilmore** and ex-Delta **Dale Comstock** — flew from Teterboro Airport on a charter to Aden under contract to the UAE (**$1.5 million/month plus kill bonuses**, pitched to MBZ's adviser Mohammed Dahlan). They were handed **23 playing cards bearing 23 names** — Yemeni Islah (Muslim Brotherhood) figures — and a bomb-making kit. Their first operation, a 29 December 2015 car-bomb attempt on Islah leader **Anssaf Ali Mayo**, failed; the campaign it ignited killed, by one human-rights tally, **103 Islah figures in Aden 2015–18 — and of 160 killings attributed to the network, only 23 victims had verifiable terrorist links** (Reprieve/BBC). Golan's on-record confession: "There was a targeted assassination program in Yemen. I was running it." The shell company, **Spear Operations Group** (Delaware, 2015–2018), dissolved days after BuzzFeed's exposé — but a leaked UAE document shows it **was still being paid in 2020**, and ITV News reported in August 2026 that Golan himself ran a **UAE-hired surveillance operation in London in December 2025** against a British Muslim activist. Two US lawsuits are alive: one in Washington (Alsanabani — the banks, First Abu Dhabi Bank USA and BNY Mellon, were dismissed in April 2026; the case limps on), one in San Diego (Mayo, filed December 2025, on war-crimes theories). No criminal investigation has ever been opened.
- **Colombians, twice:** Erik Prince's earlier UAE venture, **Reflex Responses (R2)** — a $529 million contract (seeded, per The Intercept, by a $110 million Emirati advance), 1,800+ Colombians hired at tripled salaries, President Michael Roumi publicly swearing Prince had no formal role — pioneered the model (six Colombians and their Australian commander were killed near Taiz in December 2015); its first foreign deployment had come even earlier, in Puntland in 2011, through the Prince-linked Saracen/Sterling corporate network. R2 was later "renamed, rebranded, and expropriated" from Prince after his falling-out with the crown prince and folded into the state apparatus — its corporate remnant remains a served defendant in US court. The successor-generation form is **GSSG (Global Security Services Group)** — founded in 2016 by **Ahmed al-Humairi**, secretary-general of the UAE presidential court, and run by **Mohamed Hamdan al-Zaabi**, with Deputy Ruler **Hazza bin Zayed** a client since 2019. Since 2024, GSSG has sent roughly **2,000 Colombian contractors to fight for the RSF in Sudan** — recruited via WhatsApp pitches, trained on UAE military bases, flown out with unstamped passports, paid through a rename-chain of recruiters (**A4SI** — the Bogotá agency of Álvaro Andrés Quijano Becerra and Claudia Viviana Oliveros Forero — **→ Fénix**; **Global Staffing → Talent Bridge**; **Maine Global**, the payroll-and-currency firm of Mateo Andrés Duque Botero; a north-London shell, **ODP8 → Zeuz Global**; plus **GQAB/"Mi Futuro Global,"** which also placed fighters into Russia's war on Ukraine — both sides) that US, UN, and UK sanctions keep chasing by name: OFAC designated the three recruiters and their entities in December 2025 (with a second round, April 2026, hitting Fénix and GQAB); the UNSC's Darfur sanctions committee listed Quijano and Oliveros (SDi.012, SDi.013) and Duque (April 28, 2026); the UK sanctioned all three in February 2026, alongside the RSF's financial fixer at al-Khaleej Bank. The toll includes 22 Colombians killed in a drone strike on a Libya convoy (November 2024), 40 killed when Sudan downed a mercenary-carrying aircraft (August 2025), and the El Fasher occupation itself. A UN expert's question stands on the record: "Who is paying Alzaabi's firm to supply mercenaries?"
- The individual names that bind the architecture: **Stephen Toumajan**, the American serving as a UAE officer commanding its helicopter force in Yemen; **Mike Hindmarsh**, the Australian commanding the UAE Presidential Guard (~$500,000 a year, reporting to MBZ); retired US General **Stanley McChrystal** on the board of a UAE military company; former Vice Admiral **Robert Harward** running Lockheed Martin's UAE division; **Knowledge International LLC**, the Virginia firm brokering some $500 million a year in US arms and training to the UAE; and — per US intelligence on the record — **direct phone lines between Hemeti and Mohamed bin Zayed and his brother Mansour**, with two charities overseen by Mansour allegedly converting a Chadian field hospital into a covert arms node for the RSF. The Washington Post counted over 500 retired US military personnel hired by Gulf states at up to $300,000 a year. And the war dissolved every old category: the BBC confirmed that **Nasser al-Shiba**, a suspect in the 2000 USS Cole bombing, now commands a military unit of the STC.

## 24. The Economic Siege and Counter-Siege (2015–2026)

The war's middle years were an economic war in the strict sense. The 2017–18 Saudi blockade; the 2018 battle for Hodeidah and the Stockholm Agreement that froze the front lines but not the economics; the September 2016 central-bank split that stopped salaries and broke the health system; the 2019–2021 grinding attrition — Marib's battle lines (the Houthi offensives of 2021–22 nearly took the government's last northern stronghold and its oil fields before stalling) — and then, in April 2022, the war's only real pause: a **UN-brokered truce (April–October 2022)** that brought Sana'a's airport back to life, let fuel ships into Hodeidah, and held casualty numbers down. It also minted the Houthis a fortune in fuel duties (see §22). When it expired, escalation resumed, and in **October 2022 the Houthis shut Yemen's oil exports entirely** with drone strikes on the loading terminals — no more revenue for the government, no more salaries in Aden, four hours of electricity a day until Riyadh's $1.2 billion rescue. The counter-moves kept coming: the May 2023 ban on Marib cooking gas; the August 2023 overland customs fees that diverted an estimated YR50 billion a month from Aden's treasury to Sana'a's; the IRG's own subsidy drain (~YR200 billion a year in under-priced fuel whose beneficiaries sit, per the Sana'a Center, inside corruption networks).

Yemen LNG — the $4.5 billion Balhaf plant co-owned by TotalEnergies (39.6%) — has stood dark since 2015 under force majeure, costing the treasury ~$740 million a year; former workers allege in a Paris duty-of-vigilance lawsuit that UAE forces turned parts of it into a secret prison; in early 2026, with gas prices where they are, restart talk valued it at ~$3.5 billion a year. The FSO Safer tanker — 1.14 million barrels of crude aboard a derelict hull off Hodeidah — took a $129 million UN rescue operation (July–August 2023) to avert a spill that modelers said could close the ports feeding half the country; its scrapping is now hostage to the Red Sea war.

## 25. The Gaza War Spills Over (2023–2026)

On 19 October 2023, after Hamas's attack on Israel and the Israeli campaign in Gaza, the Houthis began firing missiles and drones at Israel and, decisively, at commercial shipping in the Red Sea — a campaign that closed the Suez route to most Western shipping, forced global carriers around Africa, and made the Houthis, in their own telling, the vanguard of the "Axis of Resistance." The toll was real: the US Navy fought its most intense sea-air battle since 1945 defending it (the carrier Eisenhower spent months on station; the tanker Sounion was left burning in the strait); the US and UK launched hundreds of strikes (Operation Prosperity Guardian, then Poseidon Archer, then Rough Rider in spring 2025) that killed at least 238 civilians including 24 children in 53 days — the April 2025 Ras Isa fuel-port strike alone killed 84 civilians in what CENTCOM called a "lethal self-defense strike"; Israel's Operation Long Arm (July 2024) hit Hodeidah's oil storage, causing 92 civilian casualties in one raid. Washington's designations swung with its politics: the departing Trump administration designated the Houthis FTO in January 2021 — against the warnings of the UN's own aid chief, who said it would accelerate famine, and in a move analysts read as a trap for the incoming Biden team, which revoked it within weeks — before Biden redesignated the movement (SDGT, February 2024) and Trump restored the FTO label (March 2025). Treasury has sanctioned their financial network twice in 2026 alone. Iran's supply line kept pace with the fighting: a June 2025 seizure by National Resistance Forces captured 750 tons — six Ghadir anti-ship cruise missiles, Type-358 parts used to bring down two dozen US MQ-9 drones, a hundred drone engines — and investigators documented Djibouti-based front companies importing 7,500–8,500 tons of ammonium nitrate (three Beiruts' worth) into the Horn. The network ran on four documented channels — direct sea runs, Horn-of-Africa transshipment, commercial containers, and overland routes through the Shahn crossing in Oman — with IRGC "motherships" (the Saviz and Behshad) anchored in the Red Sea for years; UK Navy seizures in the Gulf of Oman (2022 and 2023) intercepted speedboat loads of cruise- and ballistic-missile parts, and 35% of the components interdicted in 2021–25 were made in China, 21% in Iran.

**The 2026 war.** In February 2026, the US-Israel war on Iran briefly closed the Strait of Hormuz and turned the Red Sea into a full combat zone; the Houthis struck Israeli cities and coalition shipping alike. Then, in **September 2026**, the Saudi-Houthi cold war reignited into open fighting: the Houthis launched a major offensive down the Red Sea coast, seizing Mokha and ground overlooking the Bab al-Mandab strait; Saudi Arabia answered with its largest bombing campaign of the war — strikes on more than 450 targets across northern Yemen — as Houthi missiles and drones hit Saudi installations including the Yanbu oil hub and the East-West pipeline; US-Houthi talks in Oman ran in parallel with the shooting; oil passed $108. As this document is written, the war that was never ended is escalating again — with 19.5 million Yemenis still in need of aid, the WFP gone from the north, and famine-risk projections showing Emergency-level food insecurity persisting through September 2026.

## 26. The Southern Question, Rewritten (2017–2026)

The STC — Emirati-built, demanding secession — was the coalition's other war. It fought the government for Aden (2018, 2019, winning the Riyadh Agreement's paper autonomy in November 2019), absorbed the Shabwa and Hadhramaut security forces, and kept its secession project alive as a standing threat. In **December 2025** it moved: "Operation Promising Future" seized al-Mahra and most of Hadhramaut — 80% of Yemen's remaining oil reserves — in days, driving the exiled president to accuse its leader, Aidarous al-Zubaidi, of treason. Saudi Arabia answered with its own air campaign, bombing what it called an Emirati arms shipment to the STC, retook the ground, retook Aden, and bought the loyalty the UAE had bought — with the ~$3 billion salary budget. Al-Zubaidi fled to Abu Dhabi; a captured STC delegate "dissolved" the group on Saudi state television under what colleagues abroad called duress; twenty thousand Saudi-funded National Shield fighters hold the new line at Hadhramaut's border. The strategic logic was not subtle: Riyadh, with $1.25 trillion of giga-projects to finance, had concluded that a Houthi peace was the cheapest exit — and the UAE's maritime "league" of ports and proxies had overplayed its hand. Even Socotra — seized by UAE troops in 2018 — saw the Emirati flag lowered in late December 2025 under Saudi pressure. The unity of Yemen — a question first answered by force in 1994 — is open again.

## 27. The Living Fabric (a brief chapter on the people between the wars)

This document has concentrated on power because power made the record, but Yemen is not its governments. It is the old Jewish silversmiths of Rayda — whose community, after two millennia that included the 1679 Mawza exile and the era of the Orphans' Decree, finally emptied out in 2021, all but a handful flown to Israel. It is the Mahra tribes speaking Mehri, a language older than Arabic's arrival; the Soqotri of Socotra, whose island is a living museum of evolution. It is Shibam's mud skyscrapers, Sana'a's thousand-year-old tower houses (a UNESCO site being shelled), the Great Mosque said to hold the Prophet's companions' handiwork. It is the world's worst humanitarian catastrophe expressed in the smallest unit: Mahmoud Yahyah Ali's son dying of cancer five minutes from an airport gate that was closed to mercy flights for four years — **30,000 to 43,000 people dead waiting for treatment between 2016 and 2020** (the Houthi health ministry's count), because the coalition had shut Sana'a airport in August 2016 and the first mercy flight left only in February 2020, with medicine prices doubled and barely half the country's health facilities fully functional. It is five thousand landmine victims, the fishermen of the Red Sea slaughtered boat by boat — over **220 fishermen documented killed in the fishing war** (Mwatana and Global Rights Compliance), attacked by warships, helicopters, and missiles for the crime of going to sea — the thousands held in torture centers on all sides, and the millions who simply endured: the most water-stressed large society on earth, now solarizing its water pumps one panel at a time, out of sheer stubbornness.

# PART FIVE — THE RECORD OF ACCOUNTABILITY

## 28. The Court of the World vs. the Court of Power

Every legal avenue has been tried; every one has been blocked, starved, or narrowed.

- The **UN Group of Eminent Experts** produced four annual reports naming likely war crimes by all parties and twice recommended ICC referral; its final report recorded more than 18,000 civilians harmed by airstrikes in 2021 alone, sexual violence against detained women by the Houthis, and child recruitment by both sides including Saudi training of children. In October 2021 the Human Rights Council abolished the mandate (21 against, 18 for, 7 abstentions) — the first such mandate ever killed — after what the Global Centre for the Responsibility to Protect documented as a Saudi-Emirati pressure campaign. The Security Council has passed no substantive resolution on the war since April 2015, and the US once threatened to veto the UK-drafted Hodeidah resolution unless accountability language was stripped.
- The **International Criminal Court**: Yemen is not a member state; no referral will come. In December 2019, ECCHR, Mwatana, Amnesty, CAAT and partners filed a 350-page communication naming ten companies — BAE, Airbus, Dassault, Leonardo, MBDA (UK and France), Raytheon UK, Rheinmetall, Thales — and officials of five European states; the Prosecutor's preliminary examination has produced nothing public since 2022.
- **Paris:** a judicial investigation into **Dassault, Thales and MBDA France** for complicity has been open since November 2022 — ongoing, unsanctioned, undecided. A separate duty-of-vigilance suit targets TotalEnergies over Balhaf.
- **London:** CAAT won, then lost to closed evidence. **Rome:** a 2018 complaint against the licensing authority and RWM Italia — dormant.
- **Washington:** War Powers resolutions passed both chambers and were vetoed; a 2019 NDAA banned refueling; the 2023 lawsuit against the weapons-makers over the Sanaban wedding and the funeral hall; and the two Spear cases. No US official has ever been investigated; no US executive indicted; the Italian dockers who blocked the bombs faced terrorism-conspiracy charges.
- **The border nobody counts:** HRW's 2023 investigation "They Fired on Us Like Rain" documented Saudi border guards killing hundreds of Ethiopian migrants (2022–23) — explosive weapons into groups, rape, point-blank executions, graves growing from 12 to 72 on satellite imagery — "widespread and systematic," potentially crimes against humanity. The enabling system had names: an **EADS/Cassidian billion-dollar border-surveillance network** run from Munich, and the **German Federal Police's decade of training Saudi border guards**; the US trained them too, raised the issue at the Security Council in January 2023, and then downplayed its own role. And behind the migration stood another extraction: **Houthi-affiliated smugglers charging "exit fees" at the Monabbih detention camps** before handing groups to the killing zone. In 2026, over 200 Ethiopians face Saudi death sentences.

## 29. The Verdict the Evidence Allows

We do not take sides; we take totals. On the evidence assembled across four years of investigations, the record stands as follows:

**The 377,000** — including 259,000 children under five — were killed by a *system*, not a single hand: by coalition airstrikes that hit one civilian target in three; by a blockade and counter-blockade that turned food, fuel, and salaries into weapons (a war crime by the legal definitions both sides' lawyers know well); by a health system deliberately unpaid into collapse; by a cholera vaccine that arrived a million cases late; by a famine the world declined to name; by landmines that will keep killing shepherds' children for decades; by child soldiers recruited by the thousand on every side of every front; by an assassination program run, on the client's own payroll records, by American veterans; by mercenary pipelines that ran from Darfur's displacement camps and Bogotá's barracks to the richest men in the Gulf; by weapons sold on contracts signed in the full knowledge of where the bombs of the last contract had fallen; by an accountability architecture — UN, ICC, national courts — that was dismantled, defunded, or outgunned every time it closed in.

And yet. The dam-builders of Ma'rib repaired their dam for a thousand years. The coffee growers held a world monopoly with terraced fields and stubbornness. Queen Arwa ruled a fractious land for four decades and is still beloved. The tower houses of Sana'a still stand after a thousand years and a dozen sieges. Yemen's modern catastrophe is man-made in every particular — which means it is stoppable in every particular. That, in the end, is what the evidence says. Everything else is commentary.
